<span class="label o">Abstract</span>
<p class="abstract-text">Urbanization is on the rise all over the world, under the premise that economic and educational opportunities are heavily concentrated in global financial capitals. However, access to these opportunities is increasingly guarded by a worldwide cost of living crisis, the most impacted and impacting sector of which is housing. This paper investigates youth housing precarity using a comparative analysis of two global urban hubs: London, representing a Western Liberal Market Economy, and Mumbai, representing a rapidly urbanizing landscape in the East. Assessing what the barriers to entry are to the city, we also attempt to answer the highly political question of “who gets to leave the nest and who is bound to stay”. Our purposive interviews will shed light on the lived experience of young people navigating hurdles imposed on them from above, they never had a say on. This paper aims to bridge the gap between policy-makers and policy-takers, empowering the latter to take up space in decision processes that are not designed to include them.</p>
</div>

## Introduction

Today, we are witnessing a world that produces unprecedented economic, educational, and professional opportunities, most of which are heavily concentrated in cities. Urbanization continues to accelerate worldwide, fed by the assumption that city-living automatically translates into broader prosperity and upward mobility for an increasingly large pool of people. While these urban engines do generate vast opportunities, a critical policy question remains: who, in fact, has access to these bubbles of prosperity? In other words, who gets to leave home, and gets access to those opportunities?

To understand the demographics of those who actually benefit from the modern city, we must scrutinize the steep barriers to entry that guard these urban hubs. Among the multiple hurdles, shelter is the most prominent one.

According to UN-Habitat's *World Cities Report*, up to 3.4 billion people globally lack access to secure, safe, and adequate housing. The crisis is particularly acute in the rental sector: UN-Habitat defines a housing cost burden as spending more than 30% of income on rent (with severe burden exceeding 50%), and globally, 44% of rental households spend over 30% of their income just to keep a roof over their heads.

Similarly, OECD data reveals that across member countries, roughly one in three low-income tenant households spends over 40% of their disposable income on rent. This stress hits early-career entrants hardest: UN-Habitat highlights that over 70% of young people are unable to own housing in the cities where they live. This is driven by a combination of rising living costs, precarious employment, and facility undersupply.

This is a global issue with common structural denominators (ranging from financialization and speculative real estate markets to regulatory gridlock) all over the world, though the specific administrative mechanisms, cultural expectations, and market dynamics that lock young people out of housing look very different depending on geography.

Hence, in order to analyze the phenomenon and investigate how differently it affects young people based on the urban context they experience, the paper will focus on London and Mumbai as case studies. Despite their geographical and regulatory differences, London and Mumbai are deeply comparable in that they both operate as global economic and financial capitals and educational magnets that attract vast inflows of young talent. The two case studies provide good enough external validity for the geographical area they represent. London-related findings will be analytically generalizable (with the due contextual differences) to other similar liberalized market economies across the Western side of the globe. The same will be valid to Mumbai-related findings to other rapidly urbanizing Asian megacities.

The findings of the paper will be supported by macrodata related to the case studies we are analyzing and by two semi-structured qualitative interviews. Interviews have been conducted on one experiential subject for London and one key informed observer for Mumbai, based on purposive sampling. Far from being representative, the sample size (N=2) allows carrying out exploratory research, to identify the primary causes of the youth housing crises.

## Visible cost of leaving, hidden cost of staying

The cost-of-living crisis paired with precarious employment and growing urbanization results in increasing hurdles for youngsters to own in the cities where they live. This narrows youth’s scope of decision to two options:

- having to spend over 40% of disposable income on rent
- or staying in their parental house

Option 1 is the reality for 10% of young people across the EU, while Option 2 remains the defining reality for the majority of young people, with OECD and Eurostat data confirming that more than half of young people in their twenties live with their parents across most developed nations, a figure that climbs to roughly three-quarters in Southern Europe.

The extent to which this can be considered a choice is debatable, if we take into account that staying in the parental home is often less of a lifestyle preference than a constraint imposed by unaffordable rents and insecure incomes. Recent research on residential trajectories highlights how deeply family economic background shapes young people’s housing conditions. A phenomenon that Tammaru et al. (2026) call “residential echo”: the socio‑economic status of the neighbourhoods in which children end up as adults closely mirrors both their childhood residential circumstances and their parents’ socio‑economic position. Many different factors feed this path dependence, such as direct financial support for deposit, rent and the transmission of economic and cultural capital through education and employment. Ultimately, choosing to rent independently in a different city is a privilege, rather than free will.

Therefore, all the young people that cannot count on such privilege, will be unable to take up the best opportunities offered for employment and education, if they happen to be located in cities where housing is unaffordable. With cities generating over 80% of global GDP, the increasing urbanization pattern of economic activity that we witness all over the world seems here to stay, as predicted by the World Bank Group and UN DESA. This will reduce employment opportunities outside of the urban landscape even further, narrowing down job prospects for youngsters who cannot afford moving out. Additionally, a lack of access to secure and affordable housing also has long-term demographic consequences. The longer young people stay in their parental home, the further they would delay family formation, or the fewer children they are going to have. This makes the housing policy issue one that cannot be left untackled by national and international policy-makers, increasingly concerned with ageing population and shrinking workforce. Finally, on a more individual level, the 2025 Eurofound survey shows that unchosen permanence in parental houses is also linked with poor young people’s mental health and family conflicts.

## Case study comparisons

In order to unpack these global housing trends, we will rely on two main case studies: the cities of London and Mumbai. Those are broadly representative of their respective geographical location, with London representing the West, with widespread financialization of the housing sector and Mumbai standing for the East, where informal housing arrangements and family networks dictate the housing landscape. With this simplification, we are not dismissing the internal variety existing in those geographical areas, but the aim of our paper is to isolate the hurdles faced by youngsters in Western Liberal Market Economies (LMEs) and Eastern rapidly (and often informally) urbanizing landscape. We will attempt to bring forth the lived experiences of two youngsters navigating these hurdles, in the hopes of giving their voice visibility and due attention.

## The London living scene

The youth housing crisis is particularly acute in London, considering that this city is significantly younger than the rest of the country, with about a third (33%) of residents aged 20 to 39 at the 2021 Census (Greater London Authority, 2022). London is also highly international: 40.6% of residents were born outside the UK, and 23.3% hold a non-UK passport, meaning more than half of foreign-born Londoners are not UK citizens (Office for National Statistics, 2022). Hence, our decision to interview an international university student as one representative of a very popular demographic in London.

The renting scene in London is extremely expensive and unaffordable to students and young workers. One of the main reasons is the guarantor trap. Most London landlords and agents require a UK-based guarantor, particularly for students and international renters who lack a UK credit history or verifiable UK income. English Housing Survey data reports that 22% of private renters across England were asked to provide a guarantor before moving in, a figure standard for students and first-time renters in competitive London. Generation Rent (2024) notes that 44% of recent private renters were asked for a guarantor and 20% had to pay rent upfront. Our respondent reported that the need for a UK guarantor was ultimately the reason why she preferred a university accommodation, over a private rent. In her account, she reported the systemic difficulty encountered by international students in finding one:

> "While British students can rely on their parents, broader family or any acquaintance to guarantee for them, it is more unlikely for non British students to have a British network instead. This means relying on and profusely paying for agencies that guarantee for them... The need for expensive agencies is ultimately what made me settle for a university accommodation."

Without a UK guarantor, international students often rely on university-backed schemes or paid guarantor services ranging from £40 a month to one month's rent. Those costs tend to inflate renting prices, making living expenses even more burdensome. Until recently, renters who could not provide a UK guarantor, such as international students, were commonly asked to pay 6 to 12 months' rent in advance instead. Eventually, under the 2025 Renters’ Rights Act, landlords are banned from taking rent before a tenancy is entered into and can require no more than one month’s rent in advance after signing. While this curbs abusive advance demands, it makes the lack of a UK-based guarantor an even more essential pre-requisite for London renters, even harder to circumvent to international students or workers.

Another closely linked issue is London Transport cost. Transport for London (TfL) operates one of the most expensive (and extensive) public transit networks in the world. While full-time students living in London can apply for an 18+ Student Oyster photocard to secure a 30% discount on season tickets (Travelcards and Bus and Tram Passes), this discount is restrictive to the specific zone the student resides in and pays for. It does not apply to individual pay-as-you-go (PAYG) single fares, which are very common in students’ daily lives, especially if they have part-time work commitments all across London. Therefore, even if students are willing to compromise on prime living locations, the money saved up on rent would be completely swallowed by commuting expenses. As our respondent reported:

> “In terms of trade-offs, I would have been willing to trade commuting time with some cheaper rent, and while that would have been possible, transport is so expensive to the point where living in central London or in the outskirts would have cost roughly the same."

To bypass per-trip metro costs (where single rides regularly exceed £2), students substitute public transit with walking, which directly alters their daily schedules. As our respondent noted:

> “speaking of transport, I take transport only when I am in a rush for classes. Otherwise I prefer walking which saves up lots of money... in order to stick to my plan not to use transport, I will have to sacrifice a bit of sleep (for a 9 am class I will have to leave at 7:30)."

## Mumbai Living Scene

In Mumbai, about 25% of city renters end up spending over 50% of their monthly income. According to CREDAI-MCHI (2024), the average annual rent for a one-bedroom (1BHK) apartment in Mumbai is ₹5.18 lakh, which exceeds the average annual salary of a junior professional (₹4.49 lakh). Our interviewee was very outspoken about Mumbai renting unaffordability:

> “I live with my parents, because it was impossible to find an accommodation. It is a 15 minutes commute from my house to college... There are many advantages of living with family: it saves time and money. In India the education system is very tough and there is no time for other stuff… There is not even time to cook for yourself. It’s very common for people to live with family in Mumbai. But there are also disadvantages, like the fact that you cannot learn to live independently. At my age (23) you are supposed to live independently, have a part time job and sustain yourself, but the situation in India does not allow you to do that.”

Hostel arrangements are widely opted for by entry-level workers, and can range from ₹10,000 to ₹75,000 per month, though the sector is largely unverified: of 1,483 hostels and PGs listed on Magicbricks, only 149 were verified (Free Press Journal, 2026). At the 2011 Census, the most recent available, 41% of Mumbai households lived in slums. While hostel arrangements are common all over India, according to our interviewee, Mumbai seems to be the exception:

> “While hostel arrangements are common all over India for students, in Mumbai it is not the case. In Bangalore, my friends live in hostels and are more independent, because student hostels are cheaper and offer better living conditions.”

Unlike London, Mumbai's transit system is financially accessible via local suburban rail networks, but it takes a severe mental and physical toll on its roughly 7.5 million daily passengers: between January 2014 and May 2025, 29,970 people died and 30,214 were injured on the suburban network, mostly from falling off overcrowded trains or crossing tracks (Press Trust of India, 2025). Commuters nonetheless seem willing to pay that cost in exchange for lower rents.

> “Transport is the only option... So people choose travel over living in a place that is close by to work. Community is not a problem and is not expensive at all. Local trains are how most people travel and it is very packed. People fight trying to get on the train. There is no other choice.”

Unaffordable rent ends up turning into a deterrent, pushing students and workers out of Mumbai entirely and redirecting them to other cities, financially more within reach:

> "One of my friends decided not to study in Mumbai, but in Bangalore, because she had to live alone and living alone is just impossible in Mumbai, not financially feasible."

## Policy requests from London and Mumbai

The purpose of this paper is to bring forth lived experiences of young people facing hurdles that they have not contributed to shaping. Far from being a comprehensive policy recommendation, this section of the paper aims to give visibility to those voices that our decision-making system too often silences. So, the following are the policy recommendations that our respondents would like to see implemented in the context they are living in:

- Policy Recommendation for the Greater London Authority (GLA): the GLA, together with higher education providers, should establish a city-backed municipal guarantor scheme. Since international renters already have to undergo the financial screening procedure imposed by the Home Office, as testified by the Visa Status Share Code, landlords should take this as sufficient proof of risk mitigation. This scheme would directly resolve the structural trap that disproportionately impacts international and non-local youth.

  Additionally, Transport for London (TfL) should extend the 18+ Student Oyster photocard discount framework to include Pay-As-You-Go (PAYG) single peak and off-peak travel across all zones.

- Policy Recommendations for the Maharashtra State Housing Department: formalize paying guest facility businesses, implementing a regulatory framework for private youth hostels, introducing mandatory registration and municipal inspections.

  Additionally, municipal authorities should incentivize public-private partnerships to spark investment in affordable student residences.
