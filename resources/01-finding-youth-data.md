Most numbers about young people’s lives — unemployment, housing costs, education, migration — come from a handful of official, free databases. You don’t need special access to use them. This guide shows where to start, and what to check before you quote a figure.

## Where to look

- **[Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat)** — the statistical office of the European Union. The best source for comparing EU countries, and many EFTA and candidate countries. Its youth indicators are gathered under the theme “Youth”.
- **[ILOSTAT](https://ilostat.ilo.org)** — the International Labour Organization’s database. Global data on employment, unemployment and young people not in employment, education or training (NEET).
- **[World Bank Open Data](https://data.worldbank.org)** — global indicators for almost every country, including ILO labour estimates and UN population figures, all downloadable.
- **[OECD Data Explorer](https://data-explorer.oecd.org)** — detailed comparisons across OECD members on education, wages, housing and social policy.
- **[UN World Population Prospects](https://population.un.org/wpp/)** — population estimates and projections by age for every country.
- **National statistical offices** — the most detailed source for a single country, and usually where the international figures come from in the first place.

## How to cite it

Give the organisation, the indicator or dataset code, the year of the data and the date you retrieved it. For example:

> Eurostat, *Estimated average age of young people leaving the parental household* (yth_demo_030), 2025. Retrieved 25 September 2026.

Every chart in our [Data Lab](#/explore?format=data-lab) lists its sources this way, and offers the underlying numbers as a table and CSV download.
